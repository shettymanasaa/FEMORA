# Menopause FAQs and Medical Terminology Glossary

> **Notice:** This document is currently marked as **Pending Clinical Review**. These educational definitions and FAQs are drafted for preliminary user testing and await final clinical panel sign-off.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can over-the-counter phytoestrogens or soy supplements replace medical hormone therapy?
Plant-derived phytoestrogens (such as isoflavones in soy or red clover) exert weak estrogen-receptor binding. While some individuals report mild symptom softening, clinical trials show inconsistent efficacy compared to placebo, and they do not provide validated bone protection against fractures.

### What is the difference between "bioidentical" and standard hormone therapy?
Many FDA/MHRA-approved hormone therapies are biochemically identical to human hormones (e.g., 17-beta estradiol and micronized progesterone) and undergo rigorous quality control. Unregulated "compounded custom bioidentical hormones" lack standardized dosing, consistency, and safety testing and are not recommended by major medical societies.

### How often should postmenopausal women have a DEXA bone density scan?
A baseline dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scan is generally recommended at age 65, or earlier (around age 50–60) if specific risk factors exist (such as low body weight, prior fragility fracture, family history of hip fracture, or steroid medication use).

## Glossary of Key Terms
- **Vasomotor Symptoms (VMS):** Symptoms originating from vascular constriction and dilation regulated by the nervous system, including hot flashes and night sweats.
- **Follicle Stimulating Hormone (FSH):** A hormone produced by the pituitary gland that stimulates ovarian follicle growth. Elevated FSH is typical in postmenopause as the brain attempts to stimulate resting ovaries.
- **Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM):** A chronic, progressive collection of symptoms affecting the labia, vagina, urethra, and bladder resulting from estrogen deficiency.
- **Osteopenia:** Lower than normal bone mineral density (T-score between -1.0 and -2.5) that is less severe than osteoporosis but indicates an elevated fracture risk.
